# Attention Markets

Attention already behaves like a market, but it has no price feed. Hashtags trend, communities form, and
value accrues to platforms rather than participants. Superhero gives every trend a token whose price
is a deterministic function of its circulating supply.

## Why a curve instead of an order book

An order book needs two-sided interest. A brand-new hashtag has none: nobody is standing by to quote
a price for `#ThingThatHappenedAnHourAgo`. A bonding curve removes the counterparty entirely.

- **No order book, no counterparty.** You trade against the contract, which computes quotes from
  supply and holds the reserve used to pay sellers.
- **Curve price is a function of supply.** Buys mint tokens and sells burn them. Holders can
  also burn their own tokens directly, reducing supply without a sale or an AE payout. The
  curve uses the token contract's current `total_supply()`, rather than resting orders.
- **Buys fund the reserve.** Each buy retains the quote for immediately selling the newly minted
  tokens. Full redemption follows in the ideal continuous model; integer rounding requires
  separate analysis. See [Reserve accounting and rounding](/concepts/value-flow#reserve-accounting-and-rounding).

Superhero sets the spread at 1% of curve value: on buys, half is allocated to the token's DAO
treasury and half to the affiliation chain, before integer rounding. See [Where the Money Goes](/concepts/value-flow).

## The curve

Superhero uses one exponential curve for every community token:

```text
buy(x)  = 0.001 * e^(0.00000001 * x) - 0.0009999
sell(x) = buy(x) * 0.995

curve cost for `amount` tokens   = integral of buy(x) from supply to supply + amount
return for selling `amount` back = integral of sell(x) from supply - amount to supply
```

The all-in buy payment adds the affiliation fee to that curve cost. These equations describe
the continuous model; contract quotes also apply integer rounding.

`x` is the circulating supply in whole tokens. The trade card's client-side estimator uses the same
three constants (`a = 0.001`, `k = 0.00000001`, `c = 0.0009999`) to show you a quote before you sign.

Two design consequences fall straight out of the constants:

- At supply zero the marginal price is `0.001 - 0.0009999 = 0.0000001 AE`. The curve starts almost
  free, which makes being first meaningfully rewarding.
- The exponent is small (`1e-8`), so the curve is nearly linear for the first few hundred thousand
  tokens and becomes more curved as supply grows. There is no fixed maximum supply against
  which to express a percentage sold.

## A worked example

Take a token at supply zero: freshly created, nobody has bought. All figures are computed from the
curve formula above.

| Supply | Marginal price of the next token |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 0.0000001000 AE |
| 100,000 | 0.0000011005 AE |
| 1,000,000 | 0.0000101502 AE |
| 10,000,000 | 0.0001052709 AE |
| 100,000,000 | 0.0017183818 AE |

Now follow one buyer through it.

**The first buyer buys at a 1 AE curve cost.** Solving the integral at supply 0 gives about
**437,014 tokens**. Their marginal price rose from 0.0000001 AE to roughly 0.00000448 AE per token
along the way, about a 44.8× move in the marginal price, on a 1 AE curve cost, because the curve is steepest in
relative terms at the very bottom.

**The next buyer uses the same 1 AE curve cost.** They start at supply 437,014, not 0, so the same AE buys
materially fewer tokens. The price encodes who was early.

**Buying a million tokens at different points** shows the same thing from the other side:

| Buy 1,000,000 tokens starting at supply | Curve cost |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | 5.12 AE |
| 1,000,000 | 15.22 AE |
| 10,000,000 | 110.82 AE |
| 100,000,000 | 1,732.02 AE |

**Round trip.** If the first buyer sells immediately at the same supply, the sell curve returns
`0.995` of the curve value, about **0.995 AE** against the **1.005 AE** they actually paid (the
curve cost plus the 0.5% affiliation fee). The round trip costs them roughly 1% of the gross,
before gas, so the curve does not reward churn.

## The social loop

1. Someone posts about a topic. The post is on-chain and attributed to them.
2. Attention gathers on the topic: the feed, the hashtag page, the token's chat room.
3. Some of that attention converts into buying pressure on the topic's token.
4. Buying moves the supply, which moves the price, which is visible to everyone in real time.
5. The visible price move is itself a signal that draws more attention. Back to step 1.

The loop runs in both directions. Posting about a topic generates attention, which generates buying
pressure on the related token; fading attention drains it.

## The trending score

`trending_score` is the momentum signal the product exposes. It is a field on tokens returned
by `GET /api/tokens`. By default, the web app's main token list explicitly requests
`order_by=trending_score&order_direction=DESC`; the API defaults to `market_cap` when
`order_by` is omitted. There is a per-token endpoint at `GET /api/tokens/[address]/score`.

It is computed off-chain by the backend:

```text
score = (0.35 * trading_score + 0.65 * social_score) * freshness_decay
```

**Every input is a count over a rolling 24-hour window**, capped and then log-normalised:

```text
normalized(raw, cap) = ln(1 + min(raw, cap)) / ln(1 + cap)
```

Capping before the logarithm means a signal stops helping entirely once it saturates (the 5,001st
AE of 24-hour volume adds nothing), and the logarithm means the first few units of any signal are
worth far more than the next few. Both work against brute-force manufacturing of a score.

| Group | Signal | Weight in group | Cap (24h) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Trading (0.35) | Active wallets | 0.40 | 25 |
| Trading | Buy count | 0.20 | 40 |
| Trading | Sell count | 0.20 | 40 |
| Trading | Volume in AE | 0.20 | 5,000 |
| Social (0.65) | Posts mentioning the symbol | 0.32 | 15 |
| Social | Matching comments and replies in matching threads | 0.32 | 50 |
| Social | Unique authors of matching posts and comments | 0.22 | 20 |
| Social | Non-self tips on matching posts and comments | 0.05 | 15 |
| Social | Tip amount in AE | 0.03 | 250 |
| Social | Reads of mentioning posts | 0.06 | 200 |

**Social activity is weighted almost twice as heavily as trading**: community activity dominates
ranking. **Buys and sells count equally**, at 0.20 each, so a token being dumped trends exactly as
hard as a token being accumulated. The score measures activity, not direction.

The decay term is a Hacker-News-style gravity function on time since the *most recent* activity of
any kind, trading or social:

```text
freshness_decay = 1 / (1 + age_hours / 2) ^ 1.15
```

At two hours idle a token keeps about half its pre-decay score; at a day, about a twentieth. Scores
are refreshed by activity-triggered jobs and scheduled batches: every two hours for active
tokens and every six hours for stale scores. Batch limits and processing failures can delay
updates further; inspect `trending_score_update_at` rather than assuming a maximum age.
Tokens flagged `unlisted` are skipped by these refresh jobs.

Trending *tags* are a different mechanism that happens to share a word. They may not have tokens at
all, and their `score` and `source` are **pushed in by external providers** through
`POST /api/trending-tags` behind an API-key guard, not computed from Superhero activity. `source` is
a free-form string naming the provider platform, for example `x`, `facebook` or `github`.

A rising trending score means attention is accelerating; a falling score means it is fading. Read
that as a claim about attention, not about price. `trending_score` is a value between 0 and 1;
choose entry and exit thresholds accordingly.

The trending score is a *derived* number, not a protocol quantity: no contract reads it, no vote
depends on it, and the weights above are backend configuration that can change with a deploy. Treat
it as a ranking aid, not as ground truth. See [The Trending Score](/tokens/trending-score).
